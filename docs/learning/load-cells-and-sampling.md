# Load cells, sampling and the cartridge idea (one page)

**How a load cell works.** A load cell is a metal block shaped so that it bends a tiny, repeatable amount when you push on it. Thin foil strain gauges are glued where it bends; stretch makes the foil's electrical resistance rise, squeeze makes it fall. Four gauges are wired as a "bridge" (a diamond of resistors). Apply a voltage across one pair of corners and the other pair gives a small voltage that is proportional to the push. A typical cell gives 2 mV for every volt applied (written 2 mV/V) at full load: with 3.3 V that is only 6.6 mV at the cell's rated maximum. An amplifier and ADC (analog to digital converter, the chip that turns the voltage into a number) make it readable. You then calibrate: hang known masses, record the numbers, and fit a line, so counts become newtons (mass in kg x 9.81).

**Why accuracy is a percentage of full scale.** The errors in a cell (the small non-straightness, the zero wandering, noise in the amplifier) are fixed amounts set by the cell's rating, not by how hard you push. A 5 kg cell good to 0.05% is good to about 0.025 N anywhere in its range. A 200 kg cell good to the same 0.05% is good to about 1 N, which is more than 6% of a C6's 15 N peak. So **pick the smallest cell that safely holds the peak**. The rule here is rated capacity at least 1.5 times (better 2 times) the peak, because ignition can overshoot, and an overloaded cell is permanently damaged (one student stand overloaded a 200 kg cell).

**Why sample rate matters for a thrust curve.** A motor ignites in a few milliseconds, peaks, then settles; some motors have sharp spikes. If you sample only 80 times a second (HX711), you see one point every 12.5 ms, so spikes are missed or flattened and the area under the curve (total impulse, the number that decides the motor class) can be off. At 1000 samples per second you see one point per millisecond. Rule of thumb: sample at least 10 times faster than the quickest feature you care about; proven stands record 250 samples per second or more. The ADS1220 chip used here does about 1000 to 2000 per second.

**What the cartridge idea buys.** Instead of one cell and one mount, each cell lives in a small standard module with the same bottom bolt pattern, the same top flange for the motor cup, and the same plug. Changing from a C6 to an H is four screws and one plug, not a rebuild. Each cartridge keeps its own calibration. You never run a motor on a cell too small (it breaks) or too big (the curve is coarse). It also means the expensive 200 kg cell can be bought later, only when you own an I-class motor.
